## Know Your Role presentation

Our group explained the differences between data engineers, data analysts and data scientists, the skills involved and which roles interested us individually.

I said that data analysis was the role I was most interested in because I enjoy turning data into clear insights that support business decisions. I already have a foundation in Excel, Power BI, SQL and Python through my portfolio projects.

The areas I identified for further learning were:

- Databricks and working with larger datasets.
- Data pipelines and ETL.
- More advanced Power BI and data modelling.

### Feedback on my presentation

The main point for me was **voice projection**. My explanation was in my own words, but I was quite softly spoken. Speaking a little more firmly would help the points come across more clearly. This does not mean changing my personality or shouting; it is about making sure the audience can hear the confidence in what I am saying.

The visual idea behind my slide was good, but the layout needed more refinement. For the group as a whole, we needed to keep the slide designs more consistent, especially where we were each presenting similar information about ourselves.

For my next presentation, I need to practise speaking aloud, work on projection and check that my slides fit with the rest of the group's design.

## What makes a presentation work

The feedback across the groups gave us several things to think about:

- **Introduce the speakers.** Include names on the opening slide and say who is presenting.
- **Hand over clearly.** Let the audience know who is speaking next instead of finishing and leaving a pause.
- **Use slides to support the explanation.** Short points, tables and diagrams can help; reading out everything on the slide adds very little.
- **Keep the design consistent.** Use the same fonts, spacing and layout for similar information. Check spelling and alignment.
- **Practise the whole presentation.** Check the timing, handovers and slide changes together, not only each person's section.
- **Stay within the brief.** More information is not always better, especially with a five-minute limit.
- **Finish clearly.** A short conclusion or questions slide helps the audience recognise the end.

Animations can be useful when they guide attention. For example, a comparison table could reveal one role at a time as it is explained. They need to be tested beforehand so that the speaker and slides stay in sync.

Delivery also matters. Speak deliberately, give important points time to land and bring some personality to the explanation. Avoid relying on phrases such as “I guess” or “just” when they weaken a point that could be stated more clearly. It is still fine to acknowledge uncertainty when something is uncertain.

After a presentation, two useful questions are: **What went well? What would I change next time?** The point of feedback is to improve the next attempt.

## Structuring a presentation

One approach we discussed was to start with the main answer or message, then explain the supporting points and evidence.

1. **Main message:** What do I want the audience to take away?
2. **Supporting points:** What explains or supports that message?
3. **Evidence:** What data, examples or visuals back up each point?

A slide title can communicate the takeaway rather than simply name the topic. The body of the slide then supports it. This makes the point easier to follow, even if someone misses part of the spoken explanation.

The amount of detail depends on how the slides will be used. A deck that someone reads on its own may need more explanation than one presented live. For a live presentation, too much text competes with the speaker.

### Making the message memorable

We discussed the example of Steve Jobs taking a laptop out of a thin envelope to demonstrate how thin it was. The demonstration supported the main message and made it easier to remember.

The lesson is to look for a useful demonstration, comparison or visual that makes the point stick. It should fit the subject and audience. Adding something entertaining only helps if it supports the explanation.

## Interview preparation

An interview is a chance to explain how I work, what I can contribute and why I would fit into the team. Knowing the technical content is important, but I also need to be able to talk about it.

### Prepare examples from experience

Build a small collection of examples that can be adapted to different questions. These could come from projects, previous work, education or other responsibilities.

Saying “I am a good problem solver” is less useful than explaining a problem I faced, what I did and what happened. The example gives the interviewer something specific to judge.

### STAR

STAR provides a structure for questions about experience:

| Part | What to explain |
| --- | --- |
| Situation | What was happening and what context does the listener need? |
| Task | What needed to be achieved and what was my responsibility? |
| Action | What did I actually do, and why? |
| Result | What happened as a result of those actions? |

Give enough context in the situation and task for the action and result to make sense. The interviewer was not there, so they need to understand the problem before they can appreciate the solution.

Use STAR where it fits the question. Not every answer needs to become a full story.

### Show the reasoning

I raised the importance of explaining the logic behind solving a problem. In a technical question, talking through the approach helps the interviewer understand how I think, as well as whether I know the answer.

If I do not immediately know the answer, stay calm and explain what I do understand and how I would investigate the rest. As a junior, I am still developing my knowledge; I need to be honest about that while being willing to attempt unfamiliar problems.

### Answer the question first

Start with a direct answer, then add relevant explanation or an example. Avoid giving everything I know about a subject when the question only asks for one part of it.

For technical topics, practise explaining:

- **What** it is.
- **How** it works.
- **Why** it is useful.

Being concise should still leave the interviewer with a complete answer, rather than a single word or a vague statement.

### Research and questions

Before an interview, look into what the company does, the role, relevant projects and the problems the team works on. This helps me choose examples that relate to their work and prepare useful questions.

Questions at the end should help me understand the team and show an interest in contributing. Examples discussed included asking what someone had done to succeed in their first six months, or which project the hiring manager had particularly enjoyed working on.

Prepare a few questions, but also listen for things during the conversation that are worth following up.

### Body language and practice

I also mentioned looking towards the camera during an online interview instead of constantly looking at another screen or reading from a script. The aim is to stay engaged with the person speaking to me.

Practising aloud should make it easier to explain my experience naturally. Knowing the points I want to cover is useful; memorising every word can make the answer sound rigid and harder to adapt.

## Tell me about yourself

We were asked to develop a **60–90 second introduction**, also described as an elevator pitch. This is useful for interviews, meeting a new team and other professional introductions.

The suggested structure was:

- **Past — two or three points:** relevant background, education, work or experiences that explain how I got here.
- **Present — two or three points:** what I am doing now, including training, projects and current interests.
- **Future — one or two points:** the direction I want to take and what I want to develop next.

Start by brainstorming possible points, then choose the most relevant ones. Keep these as short prompts rather than writing a script to recite.

The introduction should connect the points into a clear story. It should also sound like me and be flexible enough to suit the conversation.
